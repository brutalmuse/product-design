01 — Human-Centred Concepts

Adaptive View for YouTube TV

A concept for YouTube TV's video player: one player that adjusts itself to how attentively you're actually watching.

Role: Product Designer Year: 2026 Type: Concept UX

Overview

Not every video session is active watching. People leave a video playing while cooking, cleaning, half-listening from another room, or drifting off to sleep. Adaptive View explores a single player that recognises this and adjusts its own brightness, layout, and controls to match — instead of showing the same high-stimulation interface no matter how someone is actually using it.

The Problem

Most video players are built for one mode: someone sitting, watching, fully attentive. Bright thumbnails, dense recommendations, sudden scene changes, and high-contrast controls all compete for attention even when nobody's actually looking.

That mismatch is most obvious on a TV — where the screen is often on in the background, across a room, or in a dark bedroom — but the same interface intensity plays out identically every time. YouTube TV in particular is built for the living room, where these looser, lower-attention moments are common, not the exception.

Design Opportunity

How might a player recognise different levels of attention — focused, background, listening, night-time — and adjust itself accordingly, instead of asking the viewer to constantly manage brightness, volume, and clutter by hand?

The Concept

Adaptive View adds one control to the player: a view mode. Three modes sit alongside normal Active viewing, each tuned to a different way people actually watch.

Ambient Dimmer, quieter, glanceable — for background viewing while doing something else.
Audio Video steps back, captions step forward — for listening-first content.
Sleep Near-dark, near-silent chrome — for drifting off with something on.

Making It Adaptive

Signals

Remote idle time, time of day, foreground state

Gentle suggestion
Switch to Sleep? Switch

Dismissible prompt, never automatic

Smooth fade

Brightness cross-fades, never cuts

Detecting the mode

No new hardware — just signals a connected TV already has: time since the last remote press, time of day, an ambient-light reading if the TV exposes one, and whether the app is in the foreground. Together they suggest Ambient, Audio, or Sleep without the viewer doing anything.

Suggested, never forced

A detected change surfaces as a brief, dismissible prompt — "Settling in for the night? Switch to Sleep" — never an automatic takeover. The same view-mode switch shown in the prototype below is always there to pick a mode directly.

Transitions fade, they don't snap

Brightness and chrome cross-fade over a couple of seconds rather than cutting instantly, so the switch itself never becomes the kind of jarring, attention-grabbing moment the concept is trying to remove.

Design Principles

A real sleep timer, not just a dim screen

Sleep mode also overrides the TV's own inactivity auto-off and puts a visible, extendable countdown in its place, so the video (and its sound) stops on the viewer's terms instead of the TV's.

One control, not three settings menus

A single view-mode switch, always in the same place, instead of separate brightness/caption/layout settings.

Reduce, don't remove

Every mode keeps the essentials — play state, progress, title — and only sheds what isn't needed for that context.

Legible from across the room

Every mode is designed to be understood at a glance, from a couch or a doorway, not just up close.

One player, four ways of watching

Click a mode below, then use the seek bar, skip, and mute controls — they respond just like the real player.
