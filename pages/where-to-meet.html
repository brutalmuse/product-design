Where to meet

Maps gets you there. This gets everyone there.

A Maps feature for picking a place with everyone's journey already on the table, instead of finding out afterwards who got the long trip.

RoleProduct & interaction design
Year2026

01 — The problem

The place gets picked before anyone knows what it costs.

Four colleagues, four parts of London, dinner at seven. Someone suggests a place near her office and everyone says yes, because saying yes is faster than working it out.

Who it's for

groups

Primary — city groups of three to six

Colleagues after work, friends spread across boroughs, families meeting in the middle. Different starting points, different ways of travelling, one time everyone has to hit.

swap_horiz

Secondary — two people meeting halfway

The same calculation with the simplest input. A first date, a handover, a catch-up between two parts of town.

block

Not for

Getting one person to a place that's already fixed — an office, a venue, a booked table. Maps does that well and nothing here touches it.

Everything it needs already exists in Maps: multimodal routing, transit alerts, place data, shared ETAs. What's missing is a surface where those answer a group's question instead of one person's.

03 — What fair means

The midpoint is not the fair point.

A river, a missing bus route and a long transfer all look like open road on a map. Fairness has to be measured in travel time, not distance.

06 — Sharing

The output leaks more than the input.

Hiding someone's location is the easy half. Published travel times to three known venues can be worked backwards into where they started, so the control belongs on what the group sees.

Exact

Your starting point and your minutes.

Most accurate ranking

Approximate area — default

A district and your minutes. Routing still uses your real position.

No accuracy lost

Banded

"About 20–30 min" instead of a figure. Blocks working backwards.

Weaker comparison

The trade-off is real: coarser display, less to reason about. Banding is offered, not imposed, and research should set the default.

07 — Testing

The idea held up in front of real groups.

I started from what already exists, then gave small groups real starting points, watched them choose a place the way they normally would, and walked them through the prototype cold.

Where the gap is

how_to_vote

Maps already lets groups vote

Group planning shares a shortlist and collects thumbs-up votes, and a later update moved list planning further out of the group chat. The votes carry ratings and price — never what the place costs each person to reach.

Google, All together now: group planning on Google Maps · TechCrunch, Maps gets more social
functions

The maths is settled

Optimal meeting point work on road networks separates min-sum, which minimises total travel, from min-max, which protects the worst-off person. That split is exactly the choice I put in front of the group as a lens.

Yan et al., Efficient Algorithms for Finding Optimal Meeting Point on Road Networks (VLDB) · Optimal Meeting Points in Multi-modal Ride-Sharing Networks
pin_drop

Consumer tools stop at halfway

Midpoint finders average coordinates, then minimise total distance. A handful of newer tools do per-person multimodal times, which shows the demand — and that it lives outside the app where the journey actually happens.

Mappr, Midpoint Finder · togather

So: voting exists without travel cost attached, and fair-point routing exists without a consumer surface. Where to meet joins the two. Three claims were on the table, written so they could fail.

09 — In closing

Meeting is a group journey. The product can treat it like one.

Where to meet doesn't ask people to plan differently. It puts one missing piece of information — what this place costs each of us — into the moment the group is already deciding, and then gets out of the way.

Everything underneath it already exists in Maps. The new part is the surface, and in testing it took people about a minute to understand.
